Key Formulae

Click a card to expand it for the full derivation, variable definitions, and practical notes.

1. Kinetic Energy and Rotational Overhead

E = ½ × M × v² × k

The rotational correction factor k ≈ 1.15 scales the total system energy to include spinning wheels, hubs, and rotors that must also be brought to rest.

Variables

  • M — total vehicle mass including driver (kg)
  • v — initial speed at the start of the stop (m/s)
  • k — rotational inertia correction factor (dimensionless, ≈1.15)
  • E — total kinetic energy to be dissipated as heat (J)

Why the k factor exists

½Mv² only captures the vehicle's translational kinetic energy. Wheels, hubs, and rotors are also spinning, so they carry rotational kinetic energy (½Iω²) that must independently be brought to zero. Computing the exact moment of inertia for every rotating part is unnecessary for a first-pass thermal budget — instead, k lumps that rotational energy in as roughly a 15% surcharge on the translational term, which is a standard approximation for open-wheel/Baja-class vehicles with relatively light wheel assemblies.

Practical note

This E is the energy budget used downstream by the axle/disc split (Formula 2) and the temperature-rise model (Formula 5). It assumes a single hard stop with negligible aerodynamic drag or rolling-resistance contribution to deceleration.

2. Energy Split per Axle and per Disc

E_front = E × bias (typically 0.75 front / 0.25 rear) E_disc = E_axle ÷ 2

Weight transfer under braking loads the front axle harder, so the hydraulic system is biased approximately 75% front; each axle's share divides between its two discs.

Variables

  • bias — fraction of total braking energy carried by the front axle (dimensionless, 0–1)
  • E_front / E_rear — energy absorbed by each axle (J)
  • E_disc — energy absorbed by a single rotor, assuming a symmetric left/right split (J)

Why bias exists

Under hard braking, inertia transfers normal load from the rear axle to the front axle. More normal load means more available tire grip, so the front tires can generate (and therefore need to absorb) a larger share of the total braking force before locking. A fixed 75/25 split is a practical approximation of that dynamic transfer for a low, light Baja chassis; real cars often use a proportioning valve or brake-by-wire to vary this with deceleration in real time.

Practical note

Getting the bias wrong in either direction causes premature lockup at one end: too much front bias locks the fronts first (loss of steering), too little locks the rears first (loss of stability/spin). Each disc's E_disc feeds directly into the heat-flux (Formula 4) and temperature-rise (Formula 5) calculations for that individual rotor.

3. Deceleration and Stopping Time (Traction Limit)

a = μ × g t_stop = v ÷ a (g = 9.80665 m/s²)

Maximum deceleration is capped by the tire–road friction coefficient μ — brakes stronger than the tires simply lock the wheels.

Variables

  • μ — tire–road friction coefficient (dimensionless, ≈0.9 dry)
  • g — gravitational acceleration, 9.80665 m/s²
  • a — maximum achievable deceleration (m/s²)
  • t_stop — time to bring the vehicle to rest (s)

Why traction is the limit

A caliper can clamp a rotor hard enough to lock a wheel almost instantly — the actual limit on how fast the car can slow down is how much force the tire contact patch can transmit to the road before it slides. That ceiling is μ × (normal force), which per unit mass reduces to a = μg. Braking harder than this doesn't reduce t_stop; it just locks the wheel, converts rolling friction to (lower) sliding friction, and can lengthen the stop while losing steering control.

Practical note

t_stop sets the time window over which the total energy E (Formula 1) is dissipated, which is exactly the denominator used in the power calculation, P = E ÷ t (Formula 4). A shorter t_stop (higher μ, more aggressive driving) concentrates the same energy into a shorter interval — higher instantaneous power and heat flux even though total energy is unchanged.

4. Braking Power and Heat Flux

P = E ÷ t q = P ÷ A_rubbing

The rate at which each disc absorbs energy, and its concentration over the pad-swept area — the quantity that drives surface hot-spots and pad fade.

Variables

  • P — average braking power absorbed by one disc during the stop (W)
  • t — stop duration, t_stop from Formula 3 (s)
  • A_rubbing — pad-swept area of the rotor's rubbing ring (m²)
  • q — heat flux, power per unit area (W/m²)

Why heat flux matters more than raw power

Two rotors can absorb identical total power, but if one has a smaller pad-swept area, the energy is packed into less material and the local surface temperature spikes higher. q is what actually drives the phenomena that fail brakes in service: pad glazing, resin decomposition (fade), and micro-cracking of the rubbing surface from repeated thermal cycling.

Practical note

P here is an average over the whole stop; the true instantaneous power is highest at the start of the stop (when v is highest) and tapers to zero. A_rubbing is the same rubbing-ring area referenced in the vehicle viewer above and in Formula 5's peak-temperature model.

5. Temperature Rise — Soak vs. Peak

ΔT = E ÷ (m × c_p) ΔT_soak → full rotor mass · ΔT_peak → action-surface mass only

Heat does not spread instantly through the rotor: the bulk (soak) model gives the settled temperature, while the action-surface model reveals the instantaneous surface spike that governs fade and thermal fatigue.

Variables

  • ΔT — temperature rise above ambient (K or °C)
  • m — mass used in the calculation: full rotor for soak, rubbing ring only for peak (kg)
  • c_p — specific heat capacity of the rotor material, ≈460–500 J/kg·K for steel/cast iron

Soak vs. peak — why both models exist

Immediately after a hard stop, the heat generated at the pad–rotor interface hasn't had time to conduct through the entire rotor mass — it is concentrated in the thin rubbing ring the pads actually contact. Using the full rotor mass here (the soak model) underestimates the real short-term surface temperature and would miss fade risk; it correctly predicts where the rotor settles once heat has conducted outward over the following seconds to minutes. Using only the rubbing-ring mass (the peak/action-surface model) gives the instantaneous worst-case surface spike immediately after the stop, which is what actually governs pad fade and thermal-fatigue cracking.

Practical note

ΔT_peak is typically the more conservative (higher) figure and the one to check against pad and rotor material fade thresholds. ΔT_soak is more representative of repeated-stop scenarios (e.g. an endurance event) where the rotor doesn't fully cool between applications.
